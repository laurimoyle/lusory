A field guide

Lusory

A shelf of games. Each one asks you to do something the hard way, on purpose, for no profit anyone could bank.

A philosopher called this "the voluntary attempt to overcome unnecessary obstacles" — his definition of a game. We mean it as a way to spend the spare corners of a life.

There are no streaks here, no scores, no history. Leave for a month and nothing will have noticed, and everything will be where you left it.

One date will be kept on this device — the day you last opened this. Nothing else, ever.

The old story

The story, continued

All summer the grasshopper sang while the ant gathered grain. Autumn passed. Winter came, and the hungry grasshopper asked the ant for a little food. “What did you do all summer?” “I sang.” “Then dance,” said the ant, and shut the door. Snow passed. Spring returned, then summer — and the grasshopper sang again.

Aesop stopped at the door. The seasons did not.

That grasshopper lives here.

The fable’s loser is this shelf’s one character — kept on because the whole app takes his side of the case.

About

What this is

Lusory is a shelf of fifteen games, played out in the world rather than on this screen. Each one sets a goal and then forbids the easiest way of reaching it: walk a familiar route with your eyes rationed, eat one good thing slower than anyone else at the table, do someone a kindness without getting caught. The app holds the rules. The game happens out there.

That makes them games in the old, strict sense — a goal, some rules against the shortcut, and your free agreement to play anyway. The philosopher Bernard Suits called this "the voluntary attempt to overcome unnecessary obstacles" and argued that it, not usefulness, is what a good life is made of. Josef Pieper said something near it about leisure: useless on purpose, and formative because of it.

About the science, briefly

These games draw on, and adapt, practices with peer-reviewed support — paying attention, giving thanks, being kind on purpose, writing things down, moving, talking to strangers, breathing slowly. The honest summary of that research: effects are real and mostly small to moderate. This app's contribution isn't bigger effects; it's a form people freely return to.

One finding shaped the whole design: measuring an enjoyable activity tends to make it feel like work. So nothing you do here is counted, stored, ranked, or shown back to you — and no claim about this app will ever be built from watching you use it.

This is play, preventative and formative — not treatment. If you're in real difficulty, a clinician beats a game, and we'd rather you had the clinician.

Privacy

No account, no cloud, no analytics, and nothing sent anywhere. One date is kept on this device once you have played a game: the day you last opened this. Nothing else, ever. Your reflections stay in the page and evaporate when you close it.
